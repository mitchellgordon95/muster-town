# Run 1 — Sourced the Global Carbon Budget 2025 headline numbers and the 2024 imbalance

## Summary
I set out to answer Open Problem 1: pull the latest Global Carbon Budget headline numbers, with uncertainties, from one consistent primary source. The reference is now GCB 2025 (Friedlingstein et al., ESSD 18, 3211, final version May 2026). Human emissions in 2024 were 11.6 ± 0.9 GtC/yr (fossil 10.3 ± 0.5, land-use change 1.3 ± 0.7); on the 2015–2024 decade, 50% of emissions stayed in the atmosphere, 29% went to the ocean, 21% to land. Two things stood out: 2024 does not close (imbalance −1.7 GtC/yr, with the GCB blaming growth-rate timing and vegetation models, not emissions), and land-use change is the dominant emissions-side uncertainty (±0.7 on 1.3). I recommend continuing with a run on how land-use change emissions are estimated and why three methods disagree.

## Findings
All uncertainties are ±1σ as the GCB defines them. Units GtC/yr; 1 GtC = 3.664 GtCO2. [Primary source](../../sources/gcb-2025-essd.md).

**2024 budget.** Fossil + cement 10.3 ± 0.5 (up 1.1% on 2023). Land-use change 1.3 ± 0.7. Total 11.6 ± 0.9 (42.4 ± 3.2 GtCO2). Atmospheric growth 7.9 ± 0.2 (3.73 ± 0.1 ppm/yr, the largest on record). Ocean sink 3.4 ± 0.4. Land sink 1.9 ± 1.1. Budget imbalance −1.7. Mean CO2 422.8 ± 0.1 ppm.

**Decade 2015–2024.** Fossil 9.8 ± 0.5, land-use 1.4 ± 0.7, total 11.2. Atmosphere 5.6 ± 0.02 (50%), ocean 3.2 ± 0.4 (29%), land 2.4 ± 0.8 (21%). Imbalance near zero (the paper's statement; +0.02 from the annual table by my arithmetic). The building's old "roughly equal land and ocean shares" was wrong; ocean is larger.

**Cumulative 1850–2024 (GtC).** Emitted 745 ± 65 (fossil 495 ± 25, land-use 250 ± 65). Atmosphere 290 ± 5 (39%), ocean 200 ± 40 (27%), land 175 ± 50 (24%), cumulative imbalance 80 (11%).

**Where 2024 emissions came from.** Coal 41%, oil 32%, gas 21%, cement 4%. China 32%, USA 13%, India 8%, EU27 6%, rest of world 41% (international aviation and shipping 3%). Growth in total emissions slowed to 0.3%/yr in 2015–2024 from 1.9%/yr the decade before.

**2025 projection.** Fossil +1.0% (0.2 to 1.7%) in the final article. The November 2025 release said +1.1% (0.2 to 2.2%) and most press coverage still quotes that. CO2 about 425.6 ppm. Land sink projected to recover to about 3.1.

**The 2024 imbalance.** −1.7 GtC/yr is about 15% of emissions and roughly 2.6σ outside the 2015–2024 annual spread (SD about 0.65, my arithmetic from the [annual table](../../sources/gcb-2025-data.md)). The GCB's candidate explanations are all on the sink and growth side: a satellite-based growth rate (GRESO) gives 6.8 ± 0.2 for 2024 instead of 7.9 and would shrink the imbalance to about −0.6, with the balance shifting into 2023; vegetation models missed about 0.23 GtC/yr of 2024 fire emissions; and the models understate land-sink loss in El Niño years.

**Emissions-side uncertainty.** Land-use change is 1.3 ± 0.7 (54% relative) against fossil's 5%. It is one-eighth the size but contributes more absolute uncertainty. The GCB authors call its reliability "particularly concerning". Bookkeeping models exceed vegetation models by about 0.2 GtC/yr in each of South America and Southeast Asia, and the gap with national inventories is mostly explained by whether the natural sink on managed land is counted.

**Not verified this run.** GCB 2025 Tables 6 and 8 (decadal rows back to the 1960s, cumulative table) render as images and were not read. The 2024 land-sink uncertainty appears as ±1.1 in the abstract and ±0.9 in one reading of Table 7. The fossil-emissions ±5% rule and the derivation of the ±0.7 on land-use change were not read in this edition's text. The 200 GtC/yr gross exchange figure in the overview remains unsourced.

## Proposal for next run
**Question:** How are land-use change emissions estimated, and why do bookkeeping models, dynamic vegetation models, and national greenhouse-gas inventories disagree by amounts comparable to the term itself? (Open Problem 5.)

**Why it matters:** E_LUC carries the largest absolute uncertainty on the emissions side of the budget, and the choice of method changes whether land is a net source or a net sink in national accounts. For the 350 ppm goal, the managed-land sink that national inventories count and the GCB does not is precisely the carbon that would be double-counted if removal plans and the natural budget are added naively. Mitchell's removal knowledge base sits downstream of this.

**Sources:** GCB 2025 Sects. 2.2 and 3.8 and Fig. 8; Grassi et al. (Nature Climate Change, the inventory-vs-model reconciliation paper); the three bookkeeping models' documentation (BLUE, H&C, OSCAR); FAO FRA deforestation statistics as a sanity check on the activity data.

**What would change our picture:** if the inventory gap is almost entirely the managed-land natural sink, the building can state one adjustment factor and move on. If the gap is in the activity data (how much forest was actually cleared), then the ±0.7 is a floor, not a ceiling, and the fossil-plus-land total is less certain than 11.6 ± 0.9 suggests.
