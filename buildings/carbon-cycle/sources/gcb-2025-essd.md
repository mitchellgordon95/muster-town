## What it says

This is the primary, peer-reviewed source for the whole building's top-level budget. All uncertainties are ±1 standard deviation ("following the convention of the other components in GCB", Sect. 2.7). All values GtC/yr unless noted. 1 GtC = 3.664 GtCO2.

### Year 2024 budget (abstract and Table 7)

| Component | Symbol | 2024 value (GtC/yr) |
|---|---|---|
| Fossil fuel + cement emissions (net of cement carbonation) | E_FOS | 10.3 ± 0.5 (up 1.1%, +0.11 GtC, on 2023) |
| Cement carbonation sink (included in E_FOS above) | — | 0.22 |
| Land-use change emissions (net) | E_LUC | 1.3 ± 0.7 |
| Total anthropogenic emissions | E_FOS + E_LUC | 11.6 ± 0.9 (= 42.4 ± 3.2 GtCO2/yr) |
| Atmospheric growth rate | G_ATM | 7.9 ± 0.2 (= 3.73 ± 0.1 ppm/yr); 2.2 GtC above 2023 |
| Ocean sink | S_OCEAN | 3.4 ± 0.4 |
| Land sink | S_LAND | 1.9 ± 1.1 (abstract; one Table 7 reading gave ± 0.9, not verified) |
| Budget imbalance | B_IM | −1.7 (no uncertainty assigned; it is the residual) |
| 2024 mean atmospheric CO2 | — | 422.8 ± 0.1 ppm |

The imbalance is defined (Eq. 1) as B_IM = E_FOS + E_LUC − (G_ATM + S_OCEAN + S_LAND). A negative value means the estimated sinks plus growth exceed estimated emissions. On 2024 the abstract says there is "a large negative B_IM (−1.7 GtC yr−1)", meaning "the total sink or the atmospheric growth rate is likely overestimated in 2024."

The abstract: over 1959–2024 the mean and trend of the components are consistently estimated "with a near-zero overall budget imbalance", though discrepancies of up to about 1 GtC/yr remain on annual-to-decadal timescales.

### 2024 fossil emissions breakdown (Sect. 3.1.3)

By fuel, share of 2024 total and change 2023→2024: coal 41% (+0.8%), oil 32% (+1.3%), natural gas 21% (+2.4%), cement 4% (−4.6%), flaring ~1%, other ~1%.

By country/region: China 32% (+0.7%), USA 13% (−0.6%), India 8% (+4.0%), EU27 6% (−2.6%), rest of world 41% (+1.3%; includes international aviation and marine bunkers at 3% of the total).

### 2025 projection (abstract, final article)

Fossil emissions +1.0% (range 0.2% to 1.7%) on 2024. Atmospheric CO2 rises 2.1 ppm to 425.6 ppm, 53% above pre-industrial. The land sink is projected as a residual at 3.1 GtC, 1.1 GtC above 2024, "consistent with an expected recovery of the land sink after an El Niño event." (The November 2025 preprint/press release gave +1.1%, range 0.2% to 2.2%, and 425.7 ppm; see [gcp-2025-release-nies](gcp-2025-release-nies.md).)

### How each uncertainty is derived (Sect. 2)

- E_FOS: not restated as a rule in the portion read; cumulative ±5% is consistent with the usual GCB ±5% (1σ) on fossil emissions. Flagged as not verified from this edition's text.
- E_LUC: ±0.7 GtC/yr; derivation not read in this run.
- G_ATM: ±0.2 GtC/yr for 2024; derivation not read.
- S_OCEAN: ±0.4 GtC/yr "combining ensemble standard deviation with systematic terms", rated medium confidence (Sect. 2.5.1).
- S_LAND: taken from the standard deviation across dynamic global vegetation models (DGVMs) (Sect. 2.6.1).
- Atmospheric inversions and the ocean-O2 based estimates "close the carbon balance (B_IM = 0) by design" (Sects. 2.7, 2.8).

### Explanations offered for the 2024 imbalance (Sects. 3.5.3, 3.9.1)

- Growth-rate attribution between years: a satellite-based growth rate (GRESO) gives 6.8 ± 0.2 GtC/yr for 2024 instead of the surface-network 7.9. With GRESO, B_IM would be about −0.6 GtC in 2024 and −0.3 in 2023 (versus +0.4 in 2023 with the surface rate). The paper says "a substantial fraction of the flux anomaly occurred in 2023, but this was only detected by the surface network in 2024."
- Fire: DGVMs underestimate 2024 fire emissions by 0.23 GtC/yr, which would make the modelled land sink too large.
- DGVMs "are suspected to underestimate the land sink reduction in response to El Niño events"; few represent drought mortality or forest demography.

### Land-use change uncertainty (Sects. 3.8.2, 5)

The authors call the large uncertainty and limited reliability of E_LUC "particularly concerning" for mitigation and cumulative emissions. Bookkeeping models give higher E_LUC than DGVMs in South America (~0.5 vs ~0.3 GtC/yr) and Southeast Asia (~0.4 vs ~0.2 GtC/yr). Adding the natural sink in managed forests (from DGVMs) to bookkeeping E_LUC "reconcile[s] most of the large gap" with national greenhouse gas inventories (Fig. 8).

## Agent analysis (Ravi, sources desk)

- The building's placeholder overview ("10–11 GtC/yr human emissions, about half stays in the atmosphere, land and ocean roughly equal") is close but should now be replaced with the 2015–2024 decade: 11.2 GtC/yr emitted, 5.6 to the atmosphere (50%), 3.2 ocean (29%), 2.4 land (21%). The land and ocean shares are not equal; ocean is larger on the decade.
- 2024 is anomalous. The imbalance of −1.7 GtC/yr is 15% of total emissions and far larger than the ±0.9 on emissions. The paper's own preferred explanation is a mix of (a) the surface network booking a 2023 flux anomaly into 2024 and (b) DGVMs missing fire and El Niño mortality. If (a) holds, 2023 and 2024 together are roughly fine and the problem is year-attribution, not a missing process. This is directly relevant to Open Problems 2 and 3.
- On the emissions side, E_LUC has a ±0.7 uncertainty on a 1.3 value (54%), compared with ±0.5 on 10.3 for fossil (5%). The land-use term is one-eighth the size of fossil but contributes more absolute uncertainty. That is the largest emissions-side lever on how well the budget can close.
- The final article revised the 2025 projection down from +1.1% to +1.0% between the November 2025 release and May 2026 publication. Secondary sources mostly quote the November numbers. Cite the final article.
- Not read in this run: Tables 6 and 8 (rendered as images), the fossil-emissions uncertainty method text, and the E_LUC derivation. Open for a follow-up.
